Introduction
Overview. One convincing link is often all an attacker needs. Phishing is the most common and disruptive attack on UK organisations: 43% of businesses identified a breach in the past year, and 85% of those experienced phishing (DSIT, 2025). The APWG recorded 971,181 attacks in the first quarter of 2026 alone, up by 13.8% (APWG, 2026). Phishield is a website which provides a three-level rating on detected URLs with simple explanations from URL rules without ever visiting the link.
Context, Problem and Research Gap. Phishing is now sold as a service: the Tycoon 2FA kit accounted for roughly 62% of it blocked by Microsoft by mid-2025, until 330 of its domains were seized (Microsoft, 2026), and AI-enabled kits such as EvilTokens select their own victims (Lyons, 2026). Links carried 78% of 8.3 billion email threats in Q1 2026 (Microsoft Threat Intelligence, 2026). The sources however conflicts as machine learning reportedly reaches 95-98% on static data, yet detection weakens as tactics evolve, and humans make the final decision. No approach is accurate on unseen URLs, safe because it never opens them, and intelligible to non-experts. Phishield addresses the issue as tested on fresh URLs, shows how URL-only detection performs today and why linkless ‘zero-click’ attacks remain a stated limitation (NCSC, 2026).

Aims and Objectives
Aim
To design, implement, evaluate a website that detects phishing URLs by combining rule-based, machine-learning techniques and explains its results clearly to non-technical users as well. 
Research question: Can URL rules combined with a Random Forest on URL-only features reach at least 0.90 phishing precision and recall on unseen URLs, with reasons non-experts can follow?

Critical evaluation of key needs
Users need reasons at the moment they decide, as training alone has limited effect (Spicer and Nurse, 2026) (O3); the tool must be safe, never visiting links (O3, O4); and claims must hold on unseen data, prioritising recall, as a missed phishing link costs more than a false alarm (O4).

8.1 LSEPIs relating to the project outcomes
Legal. One phishing email can become a personal-data breach; in an ICO case it exposed HR data on up to 113,000 people (ICO, 2025). Accounts, histories and links (which can hold an email address or reset token) are personal data under the UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018, as amended by the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025. Phishield applies the data protection principles (ICO, 2026a): minimisation (only an email and a salted scrypt hash are stored, and query values are masked), storage limitation (a daily clean-up deletes scans older than 90 days), security (parameterised SQL, CSRF tokens, lockout after five failed logins, database excluded from Git) and user rights (deleting an account removes all its data). The privacy page states the lawful basis (contract). Results are advisory, so the automated decision-making rules (UK GDPR Articles 22A-22D) do not apply (ICO, 2026b). Brand checks use names, never logos, avoiding trademark and copyright issues. Under the Freedom of Information Act 2000 the university’s project records could be requested, so they hold no confidential third-party data.
Social. Phishield affects users at home where older relatives are frequent targets, at work where masking stops internal tokens from being stored and in education where first-year and international students are deceived more often (Broadhurst et al., 2020). A false negative could cause financial and emotional harm, a welfare issue, so ‘Safe’ is worded as ‘no warning signs found’, never a guarantee. A false positive could harm a legitimate business, so every result has a ‘Report a wrong result’ button. Private, unprofiled histories prevent surveillance. Simple explanations in English help non-native users, and WCAG 2.2 AA (W3C, 2024) supports reasonable adjustments under the Equality Act 2010. Training data are mainly in English, so weaker protection elsewhere will be measured and reported. Being free and light, Phishield does not widen the digital divide.
Ethical. Uneven training data could give some groups less protection (Mehrabi et al., 2022), so bias is part of the evaluation. There is a dual-use risk: phishing kits already sell anti-detection features (Microsoft Threat Intelligence and Microsoft Digital Crimes Unit, 2026), and precise explanations could teach evasion (Biggio and Roli, 2018), so Phishield names each warning sign without exposing thresholds or model weights. Explanations come from fixed templates, not a language model, so hidden instructions cannot steer them, a risk now seen with AI email assistants (Arntz, 2026). Malicious links are shown only as non-clickable ‘defanged’ text, and each result explains how ratings are made (13 rules weighted 40%, the model 60%), supporting informed rather than blind trust (Parasuraman and Riley, 1997).
Professional. The BCS Code requires regard for the public interest (privacy, security & wellbeing), addressed above, and competence and integrity, so evaluation is honest: the test set is never used for tuning, seeds are fixed, and limitations are reported. The work follows professional standards: OWASP Top 10:2025 (OWASP Foundation, 2025b), notably A01 access control (users see only their own history), A03 supply chain (recorded dependencies, pinned Pico CSS), A05 injection (parameterised SQL) and A08 integrity (only the project’s own model file is loaded); ASVS 5.0 Level 1 tests (OWASP Foundation, 2025a); PEP 8 and WCAG 2.2.
8.2 LSEPIs relating to the process of carrying out the project
Legal. Unauthorised access is an offence under the Computer Misuse Act 1990, and UK police now arrest phishing-kit operators (Lyons, 2026). Phishield never visits or probes any URL, which also prevents server-side request forgery, and ZAP scans only my own local copy. Licences are respected: PhiUSIIL is attributed as CC BY 4.0 requires (Prasad and Chandra, 2024b); OpenPhish academic data are cited with the access date, used non-commercially and never shared with third parties (OpenPhish, 2026), so raw feed data stay out of the public repository, and library licences and adapted code are credited.
Social and ethical: Will the research affect people? The core project uses public data and no participants, so it does not affect people directly. The exception is an optional usability study with 5 to 8 adult student volunteers, who give informed consent, may withdraw at any time, and complete an anonymous System Usability Scale questionnaire (Brooke, 1996) using only harmless example links; responses are deleted after marking. No children or vulnerable adults take part, so DBS safeguarding checks are not needed.
Health, safety and risk assessment. Three hazards are controlled: screen strain (breaks, posture), opening a live phishing link (feeds stored as plain text, never opened) and exposure of participant data (anonymity, deletion).
Professional. I keep the BCS duty to the relevant authority (regulations, ethics procedures & referencing), and minute meetings and version-control all work. Generative AI is used only as NTU’s traffic-light system allows (e.g. grammar checks), never to write the plan or report; sources are verified, and all use is declared on the GenAI acknowledgement form submitted with the PPD.
8.3 Ethics route
After discussing the methodology, LSEPIs and GDPR data security with my supervisor, I submit the Ethical Issues Declaration Form on the FYP website with the RP1 form before this PPD. No data collection or practical work starts before supervisor approval (task 1.2 depends on it); starting without it is an academic offence. The core work is expected to be low risk; the usability study waits for the ethics approval, with a heuristic evaluation (Nielsen, 1994) as the fallback for O4.
8.4 Responsible reporting and sustainability
Results point users to official routes: emails to report@phishing.gov.uk (investigated by the NCSC), texts to 7726 and losses to Report Fraud (GOV.UK, 2024). Links are never reported automatically, as false alarms could harm legitimate sites, and GPU-free models keep energy use low.
